Massachusetts General Court · 1996–2024

When the challenge came from inside the party

Every state legislative primary in which a sitting representative or senator faced a challenger of their own party. 278 contests, 35 upsets. Sorted by margin, so the incumbents who lost sit at the top and the safest sit at the bottom.

Two questions, two different answers

Whether incumbents get challenged has not changed in thirty years. Whether the challenge works has drifted downward — though on thirteen usable cycles that drift is suggestive, not established.

Hollow markers are cycles whose primary data is incomplete upstream (2006, 2016) and are excluded from every fit; the line breaks across them. The 2026 point counts declared candidates for the September 1 ballot, so it is comparable only to the left panel — there is no result yet to win or lose.

A crowded field is a symptom, not a cause — and it doubles as an escape hatch

Incumbents facing two or more challengers lose about twice as often (21.3% against 10.8%). But the extra challengers are not what beats them: a crowded field is where challengers gather when a member is already weak. Because a Massachusetts primary is won on a plurality with no runoff, that same crowd is what lets a beaten incumbent survive.

Why they are there

Mean vote against the incumbent: 31.7% in a one-on-one, 49.2% in a crowded field. Challengers pile on the vulnerable (Wilcoxon p = 7.5×10−10).

What the split is worth

Of 47 crowded contests, 22 had a majority against the incumbent but only 10 ended in defeat. Had the opposition consolidated, the crowded-field defeat rate would be 46.8%, not 21.3%.

The hard floor

No incumbent who held a majority has ever lost — 0 of 231. Plurality rules mean the only route to beating one is a divided-but-larger opposition consolidating behind a single name.

The raw field-size association is borderline on its own (odds ratio 2.22, 95% CI 0.88–5.28, p = 0.056); the survival split above is not (Fisher p = 0.000012). Consolidation is counterfactual — it assumes every also-ran vote would transfer to one challenger, which real campaigns never manage in full. Within crowded fields, the degree of fragmentation does not further predict the outcome (p = 0.67, n = 47).

How incumbency is derived

The primary tables carry no incumbency flag. A candidate counts as the sitting member if they won the most recent general election — regular or special — for that office and district before the primary date. Matching is on office plus candidate, not district, so a legislator renumbered by redistricting is still recognised.

Write-ins count

Massachusetts primaries are winnable on a sticker campaign. Kenneth Gordon beat Rep. Charles Murphy in the 2012 Twenty-First Middlesex Democratic primary as a write-in, so filtering write-ins out would drop real defeats. Challengers who ran as write-ins are marked W/I.

Two gaps to know

2006 and 2016 are incomplete. Those cycles are badly under-collected upstream: about 19 State Rep incumbents on the ballot in 2006 against roughly 140 in a normal cycle. Read them as missing data, not quiet cycles.
